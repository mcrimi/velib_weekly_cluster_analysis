Paris, drawn by its bikes

For one week in 2014 every Vélib station in Paris reported how full it was, hour by hour. Group the stations by that rhythm alone, without telling the algorithm where anything is, and it draws the neighbourhoods of the city.

    What the data says

    1. Two numbers describe most of a station

    Each station is a row of 168 numbers: the share of its docks holding a bike at each hour of the week. A principal component analysis finds that two patterns carry 64% of all the variation between stations.

    The first (40%) is simply how full a station is, all week. The second (24%) is the working day: stations that fill up between nine and six and empty at night, against stations that do the opposite. Offices on one side, bedrooms on the other.

    2. Clusters made from timetables turn out to be neighbourhoods

    The clustering sees only those 168 numbers. It is never given a coordinate. Yet with four clusters, 67% of a station’s six nearest neighbours belong to the same cluster as the station itself. If cluster labels were dealt out at random it would be 27%, and in 999 random deals the highest share was 30%.

    The four groups are easy to name, and roughly easy to place. One fills by day: the business core of the right bank, around the Opéra. One empties by day: the residential north-east and south. One is nearly always full: the low ground along the Seine and the south-west. And one is nearly always empty: an arc across the northern heights and a block of high ground in the south.

    3. The empty cluster is the hills

    People ride bikes downhill and take the metro back up. The operator knew it: 127 stations on high ground were “bonus” stations, where returning a bike earned free riding time. The data does not say which stations those are until after the clustering, so they make a fair test.

    Bonus stations had bikes in 16% of their docks on average, against 41% elsewhere. And 94% of them landed in the “empty” cluster. Press Mark the hills above the map to see them: Montmartre, Belleville, Ménilmontant.

    4. But there is no right number of clusters

    Four is a choice, not a discovery. The usual tests for a natural number of groups all decline to name one. The average silhouette never exceeds 0.28, where well-separated groups score above 0.5. The gap statistic keeps rising for as long as clusters are added. And a different method, a Gaussian mixture, agrees with k-means only loosely.

    The stations form a continuum, not islands: there is every shade between an office district and a dormitory. What can be said is that cutting the continuum into two, three or four pieces is repeatable. Resample the stations and re-run the clustering, and the same four groups come back (agreement 0.95 on a scale where 1 is identical). At five the agreement falls to 0.80. Four is the finest cut that holds still, which is why the map starts there.

    How it was built

    I first did this analysis in 2021 as a course exam. This is a rebuild as an R pipeline, and it corrects three things. The original added each station’s average as an extra column and clustered on it, counting the fill level twice. It used complete linkage, which chases outliers. And it picked four clusters by looking at a plot.

    Here the stations are clustered with k-means, checked against Ward’s method and a Gaussian mixture, and the number of clusters is examined with the gap statistic, silhouettes and a bootstrap. Geography is kept out of the clustering and used only afterwards, as a test.

    The map is not a picture. Your browser holds every station’s week and the cluster centres, and works out each station’s distances and its place on the two components itself. A test checks that arithmetic against values computed in R.
